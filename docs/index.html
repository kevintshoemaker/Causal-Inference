Course description

How do we know that one thing causes another? What would it take to show that a decline in a wildlife population was driven by habitat loss rather than climate, that deer abundance in the past was driven by people rather than by climate, or that more species make an ecosystem more productive or stable? What can we conclude from observational data when an experiment is impossible? And what changes when the system we study has feedback, networks and many interacting parts?

This course introduces the modern tools of causal inference and shows how to apply them to research in ecology, natural resources, anthropology and related fields. We start with the core ideas: the “ladder of causation,” counterfactuals, and causal diagrams (directed acyclic graphs, or DAGs). We then work through the main strategies for estimating causal effects: experiments, quasi-experimental designs, structural equation models and time-series methods. Throughout, we read and critique published papers, both those that use formal causal methods and those that make causal claims without them. The readings are chosen to be accessible to a mixed audience of ecologists, anthropologists and others.

The course is open to graduate students from any department. The centerpiece is an individual project: each student builds and refines a well-reasoned causal diagram of their own study system, one that can guide the design and analysis of their thesis or dissertation research.

Prerequisites

  • None required. One regression-level statistics course is recommended.
  • No prior training in causal inference, philosophy or R is assumed.

Course format

Each weekly meeting has three parts:

  1. Lecture and discussion (20 to 25 minutes), led by Kevin or Todd, introducing the week’s concepts.
  2. Paper discussion, centered on the week’s readings. Students help lead these discussions (see “Paper discussion leadership” below).
  3. Hands-on exercise, a short problem set or DAG workshop done in small groups in class. Exercises can be done with pencil and paper; some include optional R extensions.

Student learning outcomes

SLO 1. Distinguish associational, interventional and counterfactual questions (the ladder of causation) and say which one a given study answers.
SLO 2. Draw a causal diagram (DAG) for a question in your own field, and use it to identify confounders, colliders and mediators and to choose an adjustment set.
SLO 3. Explain the assumptions behind experiments, quasi-experimental designs, structural equation models and time-series causal methods, and judge when each is credible for a given study system.
SLO 4. Read a published paper critically for the causal claim it makes, the evidence offered, and the assumptions stated and unstated.
SLO 5. Describe how feedback, network structure and scale complicate causal reasoning, and where acyclic graphs stop being adequate.
SLO 6. Communicate a causal argument clearly in discussion, in a presentation and in writing.

Texts

  • Required text: Pearl, J., and D. Mackenzie (2018) The Book of Why: The New Science of Cause and Effect. Basic Books. We read four chapters (Introduction and Chapters 1, 4 and 7); the rest is recommended.
  • Also required: Chapter 6 of McElreath, R. (2020) Statistical Rethinking, 2nd ed. (CRC Press), available free online through UNR Libraries.
  • Readings on WebCampus: most weekly reading consists of journal articles, posted on WebCampus. Each week has at most two required readings, usually one that frames the concepts and one applied paper.
  • Recommended books (not required): Pearl, Glymour and Jewell, Causal Inference in Statistics: A Primer (Wiley, 2016), the technical companion to The Book of Why; Huntington-Klein, The Effect (free online); Cunningham, Causal Inference: The Mixtape (free online); Shipley, Cause and Correlation in Biology, 2nd ed. (Cambridge, 2016); Johansson et al., A Primer to Causal Reasoning About a Complex World (Springer, 2024; open access); the rest of McElreath’s Statistical Rethinking.

Software

  • R: software for statistical computing and graphics (free installation); optional, for exercise extensions

Grading

The course grade will be based on the following components:

  • Participation and weekly reading responses 20%
  • In-class exercises 10%
  • Paper discussion leadership 10%
  • “Causal claims in the wild” critique 15%
  • Causal DAG project: initial DAG (Week 5) 10%
  • Causal DAG project: revised DAG and peer review (Week 10) 5%
  • Causal DAG project: final presentation (Week 15) 10%
  • Causal DAG project: final write-up (finals week) 20%

Grading scale: A (100 to 93), A− (92 to 90), B+ (89 to 87), B (86 to 83), B− (82 to 80), C+ (79 to 77), C (76 to 73), F (below 73). The UNR Graduate School requires a C or better for a course to count toward a graduate degree, so C is the lowest passing grade.

Participation and weekly reading responses (20 points). Come to class having done the reading and ready to discuss it. At least 2 hrs before class each week, post a short response (about 150 words) on WebCampus: one point you found important and one question you want to discuss. Your two lowest response scores are dropped.

In-class exercises (10 points). Each week’s hands-on exercise is graded for completion (or good-faith effort to complete!), not on correctness.

Paper discussion leadership (10 points). Each student (or in pairs if enrollment is large) helps lead the discussion of one week’s applied paper. Leaders give a short summary of the paper’s causal claim and evidence, and bring three discussion questions. Sign-up is in Week 1.

“Causal claims in the wild” critique (15 points; due Week 8). Working individually or in pairs (depending on enrollment), find a published paper in your field(s) that makes a causal claim (for example, “X drives Y” or “X caused the decline”) without using formal causal inference methods. In a joint write-up of about 1,000 words: state the claim and which rung of the ladder of causation it reaches; draw the DAG the authors implicitly assume; identify likely confounders, colliders or selection effects; judge whether the conclusion follows; and describe what design or analysis would have supported the claim. Each pair also gives a 10-minute presentation of its paper in class (Weeks 8 and 9). Choose your paper by Week 4.

Causal DAG project (45 points total). Over the semester you will build a well-reasoned causal diagram of your own study system, aimed at a causal question in your thesis or dissertation research. Students without a thesis system yet may choose a well-studied system from the literature, with instructor approval.

  • Initial DAG (Week 5): a DAG for your system and focal causal question, with one or two sentences justifying each arrow and the adjustment set it implies for the effect you care about.
  • Revised DAG and peer review (Week 10): an updated DAG that incorporates what you learned in Weeks 6 to 9 (including mediation and design choices), plus a written review of a classmate’s DAG.
  • Final presentation (Week 15): an 8 to 10 minute presentation of your DAG and its implications.
  • Final write-up (due finals week, 1,500 to 2,000 words): your final DAG; the justification and assumptions behind it; the adjustment set(s) for your focal question; testable implications; a discussion of feedback or unmeasured variables the DAG may miss; and the study design and analysis you would use to estimate your causal effect.

Classroom policies

Attendance. Attendance is expected. Because the course depends on discussion and in-class exercises, please tell us in advance if you must miss a meeting. Participation is evaluated through weekly reading responses and contributions to discussion (see Grading).

Late work and make-up policy. Submit assignments on WebCampus by the start of class on the due date. If you know you will miss a class, you may turn work in early. Contact us before the due date if you need an extension. If you are sick, please do not come to class. If you must miss class due to illness, please contact your instructor immediately to make arrangements for any missed work or lecture materials.

Communication. Course announcements (cancellations, room changes, schedule updates) will be posted on WebCampus and sent by email. You should expect a response to email within 24 hours on weekdays; emails sent after 5 PM on Friday will usually be answered on Monday.

Use of generative artificial intelligence (AI). This course assumes that all work submitted by students - which includes all process work, drafts, brainstorming artifacts, final works – will be generated by the students themselves, working individually or in groups as directed by course assignment instructions. This policy indicates the following constitute violations of academic honesty and “cheating”: any unauthorized use of generative AI tools (such as ChatGPT), as outlined in UNR Academic Standards (UAM 6,502).

Some assignments may allow for the use of the authorized use of such tools, but will be expressly described in the assignment instructions. For the purposes of those assignments, specific instructions will be provided on the use of generative AI tools regarding the type of work being allowed (i.e. brainstorming, drafts, final works, etc.). Please email the instructor for any questions or concerns.
