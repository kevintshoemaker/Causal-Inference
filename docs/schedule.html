Schedule

NRES 701A

Spring 2027

Note: this schedule is a draft and is subject to change. Please check for updates!

Reading should be completed before the class for which it is listed. Spring break and university holidays are not shown; dates will be added once the meeting time is set. Readings marked “BoW” are from The Book of Why; all others are on WebCampus or online.

Week Topic and in-class exercise Required reading Due
1 Why causality? Seeing, doing and imagining: the ladder of causation; causal claims in your own field. Exercise: place published claims from your field on the ladder. BoW Introduction and Ch. 1 Pre-course survey; sign up for discussion week
2 What is a cause? Counterfactuals, manipulation and how people infer causes; which cause counts as “the” cause, and does that depend on what we can intervene on? Exercise: potential-outcomes tables. Woodward (SEP), selected sections; Cheng 1997 Reading responses begin
3 Causal models and graphs. From Bayes nets to causal graphs; reading a causal graph; why predictive models are not causal models; probability refresher. Exercise: draw DAGs in dagitty and trace open and closed paths. Arif & MacNeil 2022; Bulbulia 2024 (Part 1)
4 Confounding and the smoking debate. Confounders, colliders and mediators; drawing a DAG for a shared class example. Exercise: check the adjustment sets reported in Arif & MacNeil 2023; draw the DAG behind Fisher’s argument that smoking does not cause lung cancer. BoW Ch. 4; Arif & MacNeil 2023; Fisher 1958 (one-page letter) Choose “claims in the wild” partner and paper
5 Back-door criterion, colliders and the do-operator. Simpson’s paradox; adjustment sets; when “controlling for” a variable makes things worse. Exercise: simulate confounding and collider bias; explain and review initial DAGs in small groups. BoW Ch. 7; McElreath 2020, Ch. 6 Initial DAG
6 Two frameworks and mediation. Potential outcomes vs. structural causal models; mechanism vs. covariation; direct and indirect effects. Exercise: separate direct and indirect effects in a DAG. Hernán et al. 2019; Bulbulia 2024 (Part 2)
7 Experiments and quasi-experiments. What randomization and BACI designs do and do not guarantee; matching, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity and instrumental variables. Exercise: find the hidden assumptions in a field experiment; difference-in-differences with a toy dataset. Arif & Massey 2023; Butsic et al. 2017
8 Observational data and unmeasured confounders. Selection bias and natural experiments; testing for and addressing confounders you cannot measure; fixed effects and panel data. Exercise: simulate omitted-variable bias. Byrnes & Dee 2025; plus one of Watson et al. 2025 or Cardillo & Carranza 2025 “Claims in the wild” critique; pair presentations
9 Path analysis and structural equation models. Testing a causal model against data; d-separation in practice. Exercise: fit and test a simple path model. Grace & Irvine 2020; Cole et al. 2024 Pair presentations (continued)
10 Time series and dynamical systems. Granger causality and convergent cross mapping: what does each test? Exercise: apply both to simulated predator–prey data. Sugihara et al. 2012; Barraquand et al. 2021 Revised DAG and peer review
11 Complexity and stability: cause or statistical artifact? The diversity–stability debate. Exercise: structured debate. Tilman et al. 1998; Doak et al. 1998
12 Feedback, networks and systems thinking. Why DAGs are acyclic, and what to do about feedback. Exercise: unroll a feedback loop through time. M. Salmon 1978
13 Synthesis: from DAG to study design. Choosing an approach for your own question. Exercise: draft an analysis plan for your DAG. Siegel & Dee 2025
14 Project workshop. None
15 Final presentations. None Presentation; write-up due finals week

Readings by week

Week 1. Pearl, J., and D. Mackenzie (2018) The Book of Why: The New Science of Cause and Effect. Basic Books. Introduction and Ch. 1.

Week 2. Woodward, J. Causation and manipulability. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. (Free online.) — Cheng, P. W. (1997) From covariation to causation: A causal power theory. Psychological Review 104(2): 367–405.

Week 3. Arif, S., and M. A. MacNeil (2022) Predictive models aren’t for causal inference. Ecology Letters 25(8): 1741–1745. doi:10.1111/ele.14033 — Bulbulia, J. A. (2024) Methods in causal inference. Part 1: Causal diagrams and confounding. Evolutionary Human Sciences 6: e40. doi:10.1017/ehs.2024.35

Week 4. Pearl and Mackenzie (2018), Ch. 4. — Arif, S., and M. A. MacNeil (2023) Applying the structural causal model framework for observational causal inference in ecology. Ecological Monographs 93(1): e1554. doi:10.1002/ecm.1554 — Fisher, R. A. (1958) Lung cancer and cigarettes? Nature 182: 108. doi:10.1038/182108a0 (a one-page letter; free online)

Week 5. Pearl and Mackenzie (2018), Ch. 7. — McElreath, R. (2020) Statistical Rethinking, 2nd ed. CRC Press. Ch. 6. Free online through UNR Libraries.

Week 6. Hernán, M. A., J. Hsu, and B. Healy (2019) A second chance to get causal inference right: A classification of data science tasks. Chance 32(1). — Bulbulia, J. A. (2024) Methods in causal inference. Part 2: Interaction, mediation, and time-varying treatments. Evolutionary Human Sciences 6. doi:10.1017/ehs.2024.32

Week 7. Arif, S., and M. D. B. Massey (2023) Reducing bias in experimental ecology through directed acyclic graphs. Ecology and Evolution 13. doi:10.1002/ece3.9947 (open access) — Butsic, V., D. J. Week 8. Byrnes, J. E. K., and L. E. Dee (2025) Causal inference with observational data and unobserved confounding variables. Ecology Letters 28: e70023. doi:10.1111/ele.70023 (open access) — Plus one of: Watson, E. J., D. J. Glass, and L. C. Petito (2025) Toward new directions in human biology: A roadmap for anthropological causal inference with observational data. American Journal of Human Biology. doi:10.1002/ajhb.70149; or Cardillo, M., and E. Carranza (2025) Applying statistical and causal modeling to interpret thermal alteration in observational lithic data. Peer Community Journal 5: e88.

Week 9. Grace, J. B., and K. M. Irvine (2020) Scientist’s guide to developing explanatory statistical models using causal analysis principles. Ecology 101(4): e02962. doi:10.1002/ecy.2962 — Cole, K. E., M. Moffatt, B. F. Codding, and J. M. Broughton (2024) Human settlement density, not late Holocene climate change, influenced Artiodactyla species abundance in northeastern California faunal assemblages. Quaternary International 689–690: 43–54. doi:10.1016/j.quaint.2023.10.006

Week 10. Sugihara, G., R. May, H. Ye, C. Hsieh, E. Deyle, M. Fogarty, and S. Munch (2012) Detecting causality in complex ecosystems. Science 338. — Barraquand, F., C. Picoche, M. Detto, and F. Hartig (2021) Inferring species interactions using Granger causality and convergent cross mapping. Theoretical Ecology 14(1): 87–105.

Week 11. Tilman, D., C. L. Lehman, and C. E. Bristow (1998) Diversity-stability relationships: Statistical inevitability or ecological consequence? American Naturalist 151. — Doak, D. F., et al. (1998) The statistical inevitability of stability-diversity relationships in community ecology. American Naturalist 151.

Week 12. Salmon, M. H. (1978) What can systems theory do for archaeology? American Antiquity 43: 174–183.

Week 13. Siegel, K., and L. E. Dee (2025) Foundations and future directions for causal inference in ecological research. Ecology Letters 28. doi:10.1111/ele.70053
